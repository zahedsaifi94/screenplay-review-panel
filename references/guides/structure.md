# Structure guide

Each specialist gets only its own guide. Examples are invented to illustrate each principle; never reuse them in the writer's script. [general] marks craft-book knowledge.

## Principles and examples

**1. Plant a rule, then pay it off by breaking it.**
> "Nobody opens the back gate after dark."

**2. Show a planted object at least twice before it pays off.**

**3. Bookends measure change: change one element, keep the rest.**
> Open and close on the same routine, place or line, with one object, word or person different. The difference is the change. [general]

**4. The opening line asks what the ending answers.**
> "Would you even notice if I left?" ... "I noticed."

**5. Turning points the protagonist causes; the climax a choice between two things wanted or two things feared, not right vs wrong, that resolves something inside the character.**

**6. Order scenes so the cut makes meaning.**
> "On the dashboard: the same cracked compass the boy in the previous scene was told to throw away."

**7. Enter late, leave early.**

**8. Repetition as a clock.**

**9. Withholding the consequence can be the ending.**
> Rafi dials the number; CUT TO BLACK on the first ring.

**10. Routine, collapse, the "why," then awakening or a gradual return to the routine.** The descent is the stretch where the character first sees their own life clearly: don't rush it.

**11. Treat the absurd as normal; nobody remarks on it.** (A man wakes with no shadow and his first worry is being late for his shift.)

**12. The event that upsets the balance happens on screen, as early as it can land.** [general] Features: inside the first quarter. Shorts: by the one-third mark (page 1 of 3, page 3 of 10). Separate the event that starts things from the moment it becomes *this* character's problem; a script can have the first without the second, and that reads as "nothing is at stake yet."

**13. Every scene turns.** [general] Something is different at the end of a scene from the start: a fact known, a balance shifted, a want blocked or granted. Scenes connect by "because," not "and then."

**14. Each obstacle is bigger, or different in kind, from the last.** [general] Pressure rises; it doesn't repeat.

**15. The last image is the destination; design toward it.** [general] Every scene should point at it. Anything after it that tells the audience how to feel is a flag.

**16. A turn is a perception shift, not a rug-pull.** [general] The change happens inside the character and the audience at once, is planted, and the story still matters once it's known. "It was a dream" / "dead all along" tricks the audience and leaves the character untouched.

**17. The ending proves an idea; the opposite idea must win some scenes.** [general] State what the climax argues as a value plus a cause. If the counter-argument never wins a scene, the film is a lecture, however quiet.

**18. Open endings, passive leads and broken causality are choices, not errors,** if the script keeps its own rules and the form carries meaning rather than decorating it. Review against the form the writer declared. [general]

## Checklist
1. The dramatic question in one sentence; does the final scene answer it?
2. Every important object, rule or line introduced before it matters, and repeated before it pays off?
3. Does every payoff change meaning?
4. A turning point caused by the protagonist?
5. A climax that is a choice with a cost?
6. An ending that echoes the opening with a visible change (an absence is easy to miss on screen; a changed object is easier)?
7. Does every scene start late and end soon after it lands? Do adjacent scenes end on the same button?
8. Does a recurring device track time?
9. The logic of every reveal: would this really happen (e.g. an empty result where real life would be full)? Is the audience shown the same information twice, blunting a discovery?
10. Is the longest scene earning its length? Recommend trims inside a scene before splitting it.
11. On what page is the character's balance upset? On screen, or reported?
12. Is there a later moment where it becomes personal for the protagonist? If the two never meet, say where the stakes go missing.
13. Fill in the scene chart (`references/scene-chart.md`) and attach it: for each scene, who drives it, what they want, what opposes, how things stand at the start and at the end. Flag any scene where the two states match.
14. Is each obstacle bigger or different in kind from the one before? Flag two scenes that hit the same beat at the same level.
15. Count scenes and locations. Could two be one? Is time as continuous as the story allows? (Shorts especially.)
16. What is the last image? Which scenes don't point toward it?
17. Does the ending answer the inner question even if it withholds the external one? A cut to black that avoids deciding is a flag; one that lets the audience finish the thought is not.
18. Would this play as a complete film on its own? "To be continued," a world set up but unused, or a story that hasn't started by the end is a flag.
19. Features: is what follows the climax short? Shorts: does the script end within moments of the turn?
20. In one sentence, what does the climax argue? Where does the opposite view win? If nowhere, flag as one-sided.
21. Does coincidence start anything after the opening, or resolve anything at all?
22. If the film asks us to accept something impossible, is it one thing, with rules kept to the end? Does the premise need backstory to be understood?
23. List the emotional note per scene (from the scene chart). Do consecutive scenes hit the same emotion?

## Common mistakes
Orphan setups and unplanted payoffs. Planting in dialogue only. A passive climax. A slow opening. The disruption arrives late or off screen. Setup that must be explained before the story can start (test starting half a page later). Bookends that repeat without varying. Scenes that end after they've landed. Twist endings that cancel the story.

## Shorts
Read `references/structure-shorts.md` and follow its report format. Everything above applies; that file holds only what differs (one pressure, no subplot, continuous time, end within moments of the turn, length by target festival). A short film is one situation with one turn. [general]
> One situation: a woman and her brother on the phone while she clears their late father's fridge. One turn: a lamp clicks on by itself, and she puts the soup back.

Checklist: one situation, one turn? Common mistake: feature structure in twelve pages.
