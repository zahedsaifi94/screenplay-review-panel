# Character guide

Each specialist gets only its own guide. Examples are invented to illustrate each principle; never reuse them in the writer's script. [general] marks craft-book knowledge.

## Principles and examples

**1. The want is visible; the need is hidden and slips out sideways.** [general: McKee]
> "Fine. Go. I just... I wanted you to ask me to come."

**2. Show the need through behaviour and objects.**
> June keeps every voicemail from her brother and never plays one.

**3. Give the character a counter-want.**
> "I'm not trying to take anything from him. I just want it fair." (said while photographing his bank statements)

**4. Choices must cost something, on screen.**
> "The kids waited up." / "I know they did."

**5. Self-deception lives in the gap between self-image and action.**
> "I'm between opportunities. People are circling." (said by a man asking to borrow bus fare)

**6. An introduction needs one telling action plus one sharp interpretive line.**
> "Shoes polished, heels worn down to the nail: pride on a budget."

**7. Supporting characters get the line that names the protagonist's need, and want something of their own.**
> "You keep saying you want to be left alone. You've called me four times today."

**8. The safe choice can be framed as "knowing the moves."** Make the uncertain life hurt before the character retreats from it.
> RAFI: On the rig I know what every noise means. Here I don't even know which bills you're allowed to pay late.

**9. A counter-character who makes the opposite choice gives the protagonist's choice weight.**
> June takes the desk job in the same week Rafi signs on for another tour.

**10. A short-film arc is one shift, marked by an echo.**
> Early: "Don't call me that." Late: "You can call me that. Just you."

**11. The protagonist acts; the story happens because of them, not just to them.** [general] A lead who is told, pushed or watching after the first turn is a flag. A vivid character with no event is a portrait, and a portrait is a choice only if the form declares it.

**12. The story is only as strong as what opposes the want.** [general] A person, a situation, the character's own nature, or all three. Name it. It should be strong enough that the ending is in doubt, and have its own logic or stake. Shorts: one want, one pressure; a second pressure usually means a second film.

**13. Give a reason to invest early.** [general] Competence, humour, vulnerability, being wronged, a private habit: any of these works; likeable is not required. By the first turn (first page in a short, first ten in a feature) the reader should be leaning in.

**14. Change or reveal: either works, but know which.** [general] A character can change (end somewhere new) or be revealed (pressure shows who they always were). The ending must match the one the script is doing. If they change, they must start far enough back for the change to be visible.

## Checklist
1. Can you state the want in one sentence and point to its setup?
2. A need under the want, never stated?
3. A counter-want?
4. A costly choice made on screen?
5. Self-image vs. action at least once?
6. A defining introduction?
7. A supporting character who names the blind spot?
8. Every named supporting character wants something?
9. An echo marking the arc?
10. Stage directions carry emotion without spelling out the theme?
11. Does the page settle a question the writer wants left open (e.g. a final reaction that answers a question the ending means to leave open)?
12. List the protagonist's decisions in order. Do they drive each turn, or are they told, pushed or watching?
13. Does something happen both *to* and *because of* the protagonist, or is this a portrait?
14. Name the opposition in one line. Is the ending in doubt because of it? Does it have its own logic?
15. Shorts: is there one pressure, or two competing for the same pages?
16. By the first turn, is there a reason to invest in the protagonist? Point to the moment.
17. Does the character change, or only get revealed? Does the ending match?
18. Does the surface (job, manner, talk) contradict what the choice under pressure reveals? If they simply match, the character is flat.
19. Does each supporting character show a different side of the lead? Two who show the same side can merge.
20. Does anyone act only because the plot needs it? Name the missing motivation.
21. Shorts: does the central pairing itself supply stakes and history? If exposition is heavy, would a less familiar pairing remove it?

## Common mistakes
The need said out loud. A want with no obstacle or cost. A flaw only described. Supporting characters as helpers. A feature arc squeezed into ten pages. A purely physical introduction. A sudden change with no setup. An ending that explains itself. A lead who only reacts. Opposition that never threatens the outcome. A portrait with no event. A reveal dressed as a change.

## Shorts
Everything above applies. What differs: one lead, one want, one pressure; others defined by function. Reveal or one shift (principle 10); a transformation is rarely credible in ten pages. Checklist items 15 and 21 are shorts-only.

## Features
Everything above applies. In addition: a full arc or a declared non-change; a constellation (each supporting character shows a different side of the lead); an antagonist with its own plan and logic.
