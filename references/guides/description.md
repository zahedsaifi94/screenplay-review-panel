# Description guide

Each specialist gets only its own guide. Examples are invented to illustrate each principle; never reuse them in the writer's script. [general] marks craft-book knowledge.

Note: writer-director scripts take more liberties (voice, stated feelings, camera directions) than a spec should; hold a spec to the stricter standard.

## Principles and examples

**1. Film the behaviour, not the feeling.**
> "He slides the folded note under her mug, then moves the mug an inch so the corner still shows."
> Counter-example: the flat "feels like every promise he broke." No camera can film that.

**2. One telling detail beats a list.**
> "Close on the fridge. One child's drawing, held up by a magnet from a divorce lawyer."

**3. Introduce characters with a name, an age and one revealing trait or action.**
> "A DRIVING EXAMINER, 60s, clipboard already ticking before the engine starts."

**4. Let habits reveal character.**
> "She rinses the already-clean mug, dries it, and sets it back in the exact gap on the shelf."

**5. Inner states only when brief and playable.**
> A one-line inner state an actor can play ("She wants to say something. Doesn't.") is fine. [general] Too far: "Nobody ever loved him properly, he decides."

**6. Economy.**
> "Coat pockets. Nothing. Her bag. Receipts. The car, the glovebox. There: the inhaler."

**7. Rhythm controls pace.**
> "Scan, bag, beep. Scan, bag, beep. Scan, bag..."

**8. Direct the camera only when the shot carries the meaning.** [general]
> "CLOSE ON the fuel gauge, needle resting on empty."

**9. Let objects carry the emotion.**
> Mara sanding her father's initials out of the workbench, and stopping halfway.

**10. Time can pass through one changing detail in a repeated routine.**
> The same coffee poured at the same window every morning, the hand on the pot going from steady to shaking across the montage.

**11. The first paragraph is a snapshot of the world before the story.** Tone, the character's routine or flaw, something the last image can answer. A first page that is only atmosphere is a flag. Stock openings to flag: waking up, alarm, mirror, walking to the location. [general]

**12. Tag the minor characters.** Each recurring minor character gets one visual or behavioural tag so a reader can track them. One-scene walk-ons get a role name (WAITER), not a proper name. [general]

**13. A repeated image that changes meaning carries the idea without a line.** Note the motifs and whether each return adds something. When a character acts, the page should show the world answering differently from what they expected; beats where the result is exactly what was expected are flat. [general]

## Checklist
1. Could a camera photograph it or a microphone record it?
2. Does a line name an emotion the action already shows?
3. Does each introduction give an age and one specific, visible trait or action?
4. One telling detail per location rather than an inventory?
5. One beat per paragraph, four lines or fewer?
6. Can any adverb, clause or sentence go without losing picture?
7. Are "we see / we hear" doing real work?
8. Is each camera direction necessary?
9. Specific verbs rather than "walks," "goes"?
10. Anything explained to the reader that the audience never learns?
11. Are props that matter set up early, and can they actually be seen when they matter (e.g. a detail on the inside of a wristwatch strap can't be seen while it's worn)?
12. Physical continuity: where is everyone, what are they holding, sitting or standing, beat to beat?
13. Does the first paragraph set the tone and show the character before the disruption?
14. Minor characters tagged; walk-ons unnamed?
15. Do repeated images change meaning on each return?

## Common mistakes
Things the camera can't film. Telling the audience how to react. Notes or winks to the reader. Over-directing. Walls of text. Shouting with typography. Generic introductions ("Attractive, 30s"). Novelistic introductions a spec can't afford (a half-page portrait of a lead's history and inner life is a writer-director's licence).
