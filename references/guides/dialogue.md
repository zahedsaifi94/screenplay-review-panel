# Dialogue guide

Each specialist gets only its own guide. Examples are invented to illustrate each principle; never reuse them in the writer's script. [general] marks craft-book knowledge.

## Principles and examples

**1. Characters talk around the real subject.** An evasive answer to a direct question tells us more than a confession would. [general: McKee]
> JUNE: But are you proud of me? / RAFI: ...You've always been good at landing on your feet.

**2. Say less where brevity is the point.** Two words, an under-reaction or a silence can hit harder than a speech.
> OWEN: Walked into a cupboard door. / MARA: Big cupboard.

**3. ...but real people also ramble.** They run sentences together, self-correct, trail off, and use fillers when excited or reaching. Clipped fragments everywhere sound like a notification or a GPS. A short line should be a choice that stands out against looser talk. (Find the longest speech in the script and check that it loosens, doubles back and over-explains the way a person does when they are reaching for something.)
> MARA: I didn't say no, I said not now, which is...okay, it's a no, it's basically...I'm saying it isn't a no FOREVER.

**4. Short lines are comebacks with an attitude, not reports.** A fragment that only delivers information reads robotic; give it a stance toward the other person.
> OWEN: You're upset I was late. / JUNE: I'm upset you're still here.

**5. Playing down your own effort is warm and revealing.**
> Rafi waving off a night spent in a hospital waiting room with "I had a book."

**6. People interrupt, overlap and fight over who gets to finish.**
> MARA: I'm not done. / OWEN: You've been done for ten— / MARA: Not. Done.

**7. Every character has a verbal fingerprint.** Cover the names; you should still know who's speaking.
> RAFI: Fixed it. Sort of. Mostly. / GRANDAD: Mostly is what a man says the week before the roof comes in.

**8. Liars and blowhards give themselves away.**
> OWEN: People say I'm the best closer in the county. Not me. People.

**9. Repetition builds rhythm and comedy.**
> OWEN: I'm fine. You're fine. We're fine. Who said anybody wasn't fine?

**10. Comic timing: funny word last; turn the other person's words against them.**
> RAFI: You're being dramatic. / JUNE: Dramatic? Sit down. You haven't SEEN dramatic.

**11. At emotional peaks, language gets simpler.**
> GRAN: Don't go yet. Please. Just sit.

**12. Philosophy only lands as a fight or a throwaway.** Let a character argue an idea because she needs something from the listener (a mother arguing that nothing is wasted, to a son about to quit). Give the deepest idea to the least qualified person, who disowns it (a night porter sums up the whole film, then: "Don't look at me, I just do the doors"). Puncture the profound with the banal (after a speech about dying: "You want tea?" / "I've had tea."). Put the biggest idea in the smallest line ("and the bins still go out Tuesday").

**13. Exposition is ammunition.** Where facts must be delivered, the speaker is using them to win something, or the scene is doing something else visually while they arrive. Exposition that is the scene's only content is a flag. [general]

**14. Name each beat's tactic with a verb.** In a long exchange: pleading, stalling, mocking, bargaining. Two consecutive beats with the same tactic: cut one. Before any outburst, has the page built enough pressure to justify it? If not, the fix is more motivation earlier, not a smaller outburst. [general]

## Required voice check
For each speaking character: read all their lines in a row. Describe the rhythm (sentence lengths, fragments vs. run-ons, fillers, contractions, humor, attitude). If the writer supplied reference scripts, compare with one comparable character there (quote a line or two); otherwise write a line or two of how a comparable real person would say the same thing and set it beside theirs. Then say plainly whether this character sounds like a real person or like a device, a narrator or the writer. Flag uniform rhythms. Name what's missing (warmth, mess, attitude, play) and give original example rewrites.

## Checklist
1. Read aloud: does it sound like a person talking? Can an actor say it in one breath? [general]
2. With names covered, can you tell who is speaking?
3. Is the speaker trying to get something: persuade, dodge, wound or win? [general]
4. Is anyone telling another character something they both already know?
5. Does a character name a feeling outright when they could dodge it?
6. Does every question get a direct, complete answer? Flag it.
7. In conflict, does anyone get interrupted, mishear, or fail to finish a thought?
8. Could a line be halved or replaced by a reaction or silence?
9. Funny word last? Does the reply land without explaining?
10. Does language get simpler at the climax?
11. Are fillers and fragments placed where the character struggles or is excited, not sprinkled evenly, and not stripped out entirely?
12. Across the whole script, does each character's rhythm vary the way a real person's does?
13. Does any line state the film's idea outright? An early, offhand line that *poses* the question is fine; a line that *answers* it is a flag. Quote it.
14. Where facts are delivered, is the speaker using them to win something?
15. In long exchanges, do consecutive beats repeat the same tactic?
16. Is each outburst earned by the pressure before it?

## Common mistakes
On-the-nose emotion. "As you know" exposition. One shared voice for every character. Question-answer ping-pong. Speeches at the peak. Jokes that explain themselves. Fake naturalism (fillers sprinkled evenly). Parentheticals doing the acting. And its opposite: over-trimmed dialogue where every line is a clipped fragment.
