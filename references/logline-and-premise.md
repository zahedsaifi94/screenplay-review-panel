# Logline, controlling idea, intended effect

For the orchestrator, at intake (Step 0) and again at "Compare readings." [general: Field, McKee, Snyder, Trottier, Bunn, Phillips.] Write all of this in the writer's voice: short, plain, sayable. None of it is shared with the specialists before the blind round; each specialist writes its own version from the page, and you compare.

## 1. The logline (with the writer)

One sentence. Elements, in any order:
- **Who:** the protagonist, with one trait or flaw that matters to the story (not a name).
- **Wants what:** the visible goal.
- **Against what:** the opposition (a person, a situation, themselves).
- **At what stake:** what's lost if they fail.

Tests:
- Is there a built-in tension between two of the elements? (The trait against the goal; the goal against the situation.) If every element agrees with the others, there's no film yet.
- Does it give a picture of the whole film, not just the opening?
- Can it be said in one breath? If a short's logline won't fit in one sentence, ask whether the premise is a feature.
- Does the title say what the film is?

If the writer struggles, draft two and let them pick or fix. Save the result in the decisions file under Goals.

## 2. The controlling idea (from the intent)

Rephrase the writer's intent as a proposition the ending proves: **"[value] prevails / fails because [cause]."** For example (neutral): "Loyalty survives because someone chooses to be inconvenienced"; "Ambition fails because it mistakes attention for love." One sentence, no abstractions the page can't show.

Also write the **counter-idea**: the opposite proposition. A film that never lets the counter-idea win a scene is a lecture.

Keep both in the intent file (orchestrator only). Use them in Stage 2: does the climax prove the idea; where does the counter-idea win; does any line *state* the idea (a line that poses the question early is fine; a line that answers it is a flag).

## 3. Intended effect (one word)

Ask the writer: in one word, what should the audience feel at the end? (Ache, relief, unease, laughter, recognition.) Keep it with the intent. The Audience Reader is asked the same question blind; compare.

## 4. Festival targets and production scale (shorts especially)

Ask: which festivals or competitions, if known; prestige / formal or audience / story; and the scale the writer can actually make (locations, cast size, budget tier). Pass these to the Programmer / Reader only, in the brief. They are not shared with the other specialists.

## 5. At "Compare readings"

Lay side by side:
- The writer's logline vs. each specialist's one-sentence reading (who, wants what, against what).
- The writer's controlling idea vs. what each specialist says the film is about.
- The writer's intended effect vs. the Audience Reader's one word.
- The Audience Reader's early-stop lines (what they thought the film was about after half a page / page 10).

What matches is landing. What's missing from every reading is too hidden. What a specialist quotes a line for is too stated. Report it in that order.
