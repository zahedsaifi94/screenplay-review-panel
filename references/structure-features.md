# Structure for features

Read this only when FORMAT = feature. Use it with the Structure guide's principles and checklist; this file adds the feature frame. [general: Field, McKee, Snyder, Trottier.] The landmarks below are a diagnostic map, not a grade: percentages are ranges, never a page; a missing beat is a question for the writer; a confirmed choice goes in the decisions file and isn't raised again.

## Form first

Before applying any of this, confirm the form the writer declared at intake:
- **Classical (closed):** active lead, causal plot, closed ending. Everything below applies.
- **Minimal (open):** passive or reactive leads, open endings, inner conflict foregrounded are choices. Apply the landmarks loosely; judge whether the script keeps its own rules and whether the openness is earned rather than a shrug.
- **Anti-structure:** coincidence, broken time, inconsistent reality are the point. Judge consistency of the game, not the landmarks.

## The landmark map

Fill this in with page numbers from the script, then compare against the ranges. Report placement as a diagnostic ("the first turn lands at 38%, late; here's what the pages before it are doing"), not as a fault.

| Landmark | Where it usually lands | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Opening image | first page | A snapshot of the world and the lead before the story; sets the tone the last image answers |
| Inciting incident | as early as it can land; no later than ~25% | Upsets the balance of the lead's life, on screen. A separate "becomes personal" moment may follow |
| First-act turn | 20–25% | The lead commits; the story can't go back. Caused by the lead |
| Midpoint | ~50% | A reversal or a shift from reacting to acting; often a false win or a false loss; stakes rise |
| Second-act low | 70–75% | The worst point; the old way of doing things fails |
| Crisis | just before the climax | The dilemma: a choice between two things wanted, or two things feared, not right vs wrong |
| Climax | 85–95% | The choice made, on screen, with cost; proves the film's idea |
| Resolution | the last few pages | Short. Answers the want (won, lost, given up). Never explains |

Optional elements (ask whether they're there; don't require them): a B-story or subplot that carries the idea and crosses the main line at the midpoint; "pinches" in each half of the second act that remind us of the central conflict; a theme posed early by someone other than the lead.

## Sequences

A feature is 8–12 sequences of 2–5 scenes, each with its own beginning, middle and end and its own question. Mark them. A sequence with no question of its own is the first candidate for cuts. Debate items and the walkthrough are grouped by sequence.

## Middle-act checks

- **Escalation:** each obstacle bigger or different in kind. Two sequences hitting the same beat at the same level sag.
- **Vanished threads:** a character or question that disappears for 25–30 pages and returns unannounced.
- **Passive stretches:** three or more scenes in a row where the lead doesn't drive. After the first turn, a flag.
- **Emotional range:** list the emotional note per sequence; long runs of the same note.
- **Exposition:** should arrive as ammunition (someone uses a fact to win something) or while the scene is doing something else visually. A scene whose only content is information is a flag.
- **Coincidence:** may start trouble early; may never resolve it.

## Character at feature length

- A full arc (change) or a declared non-change (reveal). Either way the ending matches.
- A constellation: each supporting character shows a different side of the lead; two who show the same side can merge.
- The antagonist (person, situation or self) has its own plan and logic, and is strong enough that the ending is in doubt.

## Length

90–120 pages; comedies toward 90–100; dramas may run to ~120. Over 120, raise once. Page ≈ minute. Ask whether this is the shortest version of this story.

## Reference library

Ask for 3–5 features in the same genre and form. For two of them, locate the first-act turn and the midpoint so landmark placement is compared against real scripts, not only the table.

## Output scaling (features)

- Sequence by sequence in the review message; the full line-level list goes in the one output file the orchestrator names in the brief.
- Top ten instead of top five.
- Attach the landmark map and the scene chart.
- Tiered presentation to the writer: **Tier 1** structure and character (landmarks, drive, opposition, ending); **Tier 2** scene-level (cuts, merges, order); **Tier 3** lines, by sequence, approved in batches. Tier 1 is debated and settled before Tier 2 opens.

## Report format (Structure specialist, features)

1. Form: confirmed or disputed.
2. Landmark map with pages and percentages, and one line on each placement.
3. Sequence list with each sequence's question.
4. Scene chart findings (see `references/scene-chart.md`).
5. Middle-act checks: escalation, vanished threads, passive stretches, emotional range.
6. Ending: crisis as dilemma; what the climax argues; length of the resolution.
7. Then the sequence-by-sequence notes, with the full line list in a file.
