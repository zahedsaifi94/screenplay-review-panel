# Structure for shorts

Read this only when FORMAT = short. Use it with the Structure guide's principles and checklist; this file adds the short-film frame. [general: Field, McKee, Snyder, Trottier on proportion; Bunn, Phillips and published festival-programmer guidance on shorts.] Nothing here is a grade: pages are ranges; a missing beat is a question for the writer; a confirmed choice goes in the decisions file and isn't raised again.

## Most of the craft is the same

Almost everything in the Structure and Character guides applies to a short exactly as it does to a feature: something upsets the balance early and on screen; every scene turns; pressure rises; the lead drives; the opposition is real; the crisis is a genuine choice; the climax proves the idea; the resolution is brief and never explains; the last image answers the first. The feature landmarks exist in a short too, in proportion. What changes is scale and economy.

## Landmarks for a short, by proportion

Page ≈ minute. Fill in the script's actual pages, compare against the range, and report placement as a diagnostic ("the disruption lands on page 5 of 10, late; here's what pages 1–4 are doing"), never as a fault.

| Landmark | What it does | Where | 5 pp | 10 pp | 15 pp | 20 pp |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | Who it's about, what they want, what kind of film this is. Screeners decide in the first minute. | first half page | ½ | ½ | ½–1 | ½–1 |
| Disruption | The event that upsets the balance, on screen (a line is enough). | by ~30% | 1–1½ | 2–3 | 3–4 | 4–6 |
| Commitment | The lead acts on it; the story can't go back. Often the same beat as the disruption in a very short script. | ~25–35% | 1½ | 3 | 4–5 | 6–7 |
| Midpoint shift | Reacting becomes acting, or a false win / false loss raises the pressure. A beat, not a scene. | ~50% | 2½ | 5 | 7–8 | 10 |
| Low point | Pressure peaks; the old way fails. | ~70–80% | 3½–4 | 7–8 | 10–12 | 14–16 |
| Crisis and climax | The genuine choice (two things wanted, or two feared), made on screen, with cost; the perception shift the character and audience share. | ~85–95% | 4–4½ | 8½–9½ | 13–14 | 17–19 |
| Last image | Answers the first. The script ends within moments of the turn. | final lines | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 |

Minimum arc for the shortest scripts (1–5 pages): image → disruption → choice → reversal → consequence → mirror image. Any of these can be a single line.

## The few things that differ from a feature

| | Short | Why |
|---|---|---|
| One idea, one pressure | One want, one pressure, one irrevocable moment. A second pressure or a subplot is usually a second film. | No pages to pay a second thread off. |
| Landmarks are beats, not scenes | The midpoint, low point and resolution happen as lines or moments. Don't ask for them as scenes; don't ask for a B-story or pinches. | Compression. |
| Exposition | Should need almost none. If it's heavy, the premise is too big or the central pairing too familiar. | A short can't afford to explain its world. |
| Time | As continuous as the story allows. Flashbacks, montages and dreams are questions ("is the story starting in the right place?"), not tools. | Each cut in time costs setup. |
| Ending | Within moments of the turn. Progression (A to B) is enough; a transformation is rarely credible in ten pages. The inner question is answered; the external one may stay open. | No pages for a resolution scene. |
| Scenes and locations | Fewer. Ask of every pair: could these be one? | Economy and producibility. |
| Length | Judged against the target festivals, not a fixed range; credits count. | Programmers build blocks. |
| Stand-alone | Must play as a complete film, not a first act, trailer or proof of concept. | Second most-cited rejection reason. |

## Name the moment

Write down the single irrevocable moment the script exists to deliver. Is it where the story turns, or the start of a bigger story? Does every scene point at it? If it can't be named, say so; that is usually the note.

## Opening and ending drills

- Could it start half a page later? If page one is explanation, the film starts on page two.
- Stock openings to flag: waking up, alarm clock, mirror, walking to the location, a phone call that only delivers information.
- Know the last image first. Cut on the peak: no tail, no reaction shot telling the audience how to feel, no line that explains.
- A stop is the commonest failure. The ending must *feel* complete even when open; "cut to black because I didn't know how to end it" is not an ending.

## Report format (Structure specialist, shorts)

1. Landmark map: the script's pages against the table, one line on each placement.
2. The moment: named; does every scene point at it.
3. Opening: page of the hook; page of the disruption; could it start later.
4. Scene chart findings (see `references/scene-chart.md`).
5. Ending: conclusion or stop; shift or rug-pull; what follows the turn.
6. Then the usual scene-by-scene notes.
